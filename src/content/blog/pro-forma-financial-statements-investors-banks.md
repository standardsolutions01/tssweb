Whether you are raising your first investment round, applying for a bank loan or preparing a business plan, someone is going to ask for your numbers. And for most early-stage businesses, those numbers will be **pro forma financial statements**: a view of what the business will look like if your plan works.

Good pro formas build confidence. Weak ones raise more questions than they answer. This guide explains what pro forma statements are, what investors and banks expect to see, and how to avoid the mistakes that undermine an otherwise strong plan.

## What are pro forma financial statements?

"Pro forma" is Latin for "as a matter of form". In finance, pro forma statements show a company's results or financial position **based on assumptions** rather than on completed historical transactions.

They are commonly used for:

- **Startup fundraising** — showing how the business will grow and what the investment will achieve.
- **Bank loans** — demonstrating that the business can generate enough cash to repay.
- **Business plans** — including plans submitted with some visa and grant applications.
- **Acquisitions and restructures** — showing what combined or adjusted results would look like.
- **Internal planning** — testing a new product, location or pricing strategy before committing.

The word can describe two different things: forward-looking **projections**, and historical figures **adjusted** to show the effect of a specific event, such as an acquisition. For most small businesses and startups, it means projections.

## The three core statements

A credible pro forma package contains three linked statements.

### 1. Projected profit and loss (income statement)

Shows expected revenue, costs and profit over time. This is where most attention goes — but on its own, it is not enough.

### 2. Projected balance sheet

Shows what the business will own (assets), owe (liabilities) and the owners' stake (equity) at each point. It reveals the impact of growth on working capital, fixed assets and borrowing.

### 3. Projected cash flow statement

Shows how cash actually moves. A profitable business can still run out of cash if customers pay slowly, stock builds up or capital spending is front-loaded. For many readers, especially lenders, **cash flow is the most important statement**.

The three must be **integrated**: profit flows into equity, cash movements reconcile to the balance sheet, and the balance sheet balances in every period. An unbalanced or disconnected model is an immediate red flag.

## What investors look for

Early-stage investors know your projections will not be exactly right. What they are really assessing is **how you think**.

### Clear, testable assumptions

Investors want to see the drivers behind the numbers: number of customers, conversion rates, average order value, churn, pricing, sales team productivity. A model built "bottom-up" from these drivers is far more convincing than one that simply assumes a percentage of a large market.

### Realistic growth

Rapid growth is fine if it is explained. A "hockey stick" that appears from nowhere, with no change in sales capacity, marketing spend or product, will be discounted.

### Unit economics

Metrics such as customer acquisition cost (CAC), lifetime value (LTV), gross margin per unit and payback period show whether growth creates value or just burns cash.

### Burn and runway

How much cash will the business consume each month, and how long will the investment last? Investors want to see that the round gets you to a meaningful milestone — and ideally to the next fundraising point with time to spare.

### Use of funds

A simple breakdown of how the money will be spent — product, hiring, marketing, working capital — and how that spending connects to the growth in the model.

### Scenarios

Base, upside and downside cases show you have considered what could go wrong and how you would respond.

## What banks look for

Banks are less interested in upside and more interested in **certainty of repayment**. Their focus is different.

### Cash flow and repayment capacity

Lenders want to see that operating cash flow comfortably covers loan repayments. A common measure is the **debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)** — cash available for debt service divided by the repayments due. Each lender sets its own thresholds.

### Conservative assumptions

Optimistic sales projections that work for an investor pitch may hurt a loan application. Banks prefer assumptions grounded in historical performance, signed contracts or clear market evidence.

### Historical accounts

Where the business has trading history, banks expect projections to connect logically to past results. A sudden jump in margin or revenue needs a clear explanation.

### Security and covenants

Banks may ask for security or personal guarantees, and may set covenants (for example, minimum cash balances or maximum leverage). Your model should show you can meet them under the downside case.

## How to build credible assumptions

Assumptions are the foundation of any pro forma. A few principles make them more credible:

1. **Start from what you know.** Use actual results, pilot data, signed letters of intent and real supplier quotes wherever possible.
2. **Separate inputs from calculations.** Keep all assumptions on one clearly labelled sheet so readers can review and change them.
3. **Link revenue to capacity.** If revenue grows, sales staff, marketing spend, servers or production capacity should grow too.
4. **Model working capital.** Include realistic customer payment terms, supplier terms and stock levels.
5. **Include all costs.** Salaries with employer costs, software, insurance, professional fees, taxes and contingency.
6. **Document your reasoning.** A short note beside each key assumption explaining where it came from goes a long way.

## How far ahead should you project?

| Purpose | Typical period | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Startup fundraising | 3–5 years | Monthly for 12–24 months, then annual |
| Bank loan | Loan term, often 3–5 years | Monthly or quarterly for year 1 |
| Internal budget | 12 months | Monthly |
| Business plan | 3 years | Monthly year 1, then annual |

## Common mistakes that undermine pro formas

- **Top-down revenue:** "If we capture just 1% of the market…" is not a forecast.
- **No cash flow statement:** profit is not cash.
- **Balance sheet that doesn't balance** or isn't linked at all.
- **Ignoring tax and VAT** timing.
- **Hard-coded numbers** buried in formulas, making the model impossible to update.
- **Single scenario only**, with no sense of risk.
- **Hiring plans that don't match growth** — revenue triples while headcount stays flat.
- **Formatting chaos** — readers lose trust if they can't follow the model.

## Presenting your pro formas

The model is only part of the job. Most readers will first see a summary. Prepare:

- A one-page summary of key outputs: revenue, gross margin, EBITDA, cash balance, runway or DSCR.
- Simple charts showing revenue growth and cash over time.
- A short list of key assumptions and their sources.
- The full model available for detailed review.

Be ready to walk through it confidently. The ability to explain why each number is what it is often matters more than the numbers themselves.
